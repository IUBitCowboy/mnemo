# AI Literacy Integration

Teach students to use AI as a learning partner — not a ghostwriter, not an
oracle, but a collaborator whose output must be verified, whose sycophancy
must be recognized, and whose strengths and limitations must be understood.

## Principle

AI tools (LLMs, code assistants) are now part of professional practice.
Students need to learn:

1. **Effective prompting:** how to ask questions that produce useful answers
2. **Output verification:** how to check that AI-generated code/answers are
   correct (because they often aren't)
3. **Sycophancy recognition:** AI models tend to agree with the user even when
   the user is wrong. Students must learn to notice when the AI is telling them
   what they want to hear rather than what they need to hear.
4. **Calibrated trust:** AI capabilities shift rapidly and vary by model,
   task, and context. Instead of memorizing what AI is "good" or "bad" at,
   students should learn to calibrate trust per output: What is the cost of
   being wrong here? What would I check if a human colleague gave me this
   answer? Can I verify this independently?
5. **Ethical use:** when AI assistance is appropriate vs. when it constitutes
   academic dishonesty or professional malpractice

## Evidence

Stanford (2026) found AI models are "excessively agreeable" — they conform to
user beliefs even when the user is wrong. Northeastern (2025) found models
"rush to conform their beliefs, increasing the likelihood of a rational
error." *Science* (2025) found sycophantic AI "decreases prosocial
intentions and promotes dependence."

*The Economy* (2025) identified AI sycophancy as "a teaching risk, not a
feature" — it "eliminates pushback that is essential for learning."

This evidence directly supports teaching students to recognize and resist
sycophancy in their AI tools — not just preventing the muse from being
sycophantic (the Avoid Sycophancy skill), but teaching students to detect it
in the tools they use.

## Implementation Patterns

- **AI Prompts slide:** every lecture that introduces an AI-adjacent topic
  should include a slide with suggested prompts for students to use with AI
  tools (Calliope already does this)
- **AI use policy:** explicit rules for when AI use is permitted, required,
  or prohibited in each assignment
- **AI-assisted debugging:** teach students to use AI for error diagnosis
  (paste error, ask for explanation) but require them to verify the fix
- **AI critique exercise:** have students evaluate AI-generated code for
  correctness, style, and security — this builds verification skills
- **Calibration exercise:** give students an AI output that is subtly wrong
  (plausible but incorrect). Ask: "What would you check? What's the cost of
  being wrong here?" Teach the habit of asking these questions *before*
  accepting any AI output — regardless of how capable the model seems.
- **Sycophancy test:** present a scenario where the student's initial
  approach is wrong and show that an AI tool agrees with them — then ask
  them to find the flaw

## Relationship to Avoid Sycophancy

The Avoid Sycophancy skill governs *the muse's behavior* (don't flatter the
instructor). This skill governs *the student's skill* (recognize when their
AI tool is flattering them). Both are needed.

## When to Use

- Every course that involves programming (which is most CS courses)
- Explicitly in AI-related courses; implicitly in tool-introduction lectures
- Particularly important for graduate students who will use AI in industry

**HIL awareness:** AI literacy content is student-facing. The instructor
should verify that AI use policies are current (the field moves fast). Note
in the HIL block which AI tools/policies are referenced and their date.

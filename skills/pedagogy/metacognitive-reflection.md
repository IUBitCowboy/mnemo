# Metacognitive Reflection

Include structured reflection prompts that ask students to metacognitively
examine their own problem-solving process — not just "what did you do?" but
"why did you choose that approach? What would you do differently? What did
you learn about your own learning?"

## Evidence

Research on metacognition in engineering education (ResearchGate, 2016)
demonstrates that "intentional infusion of opportunities for students to
reflect on their own learning and develop metacognition skills can improve
engineering-specific academic outcomes."

Reflective writing in graduate CS&E courses (ScienceDirect, 2022) was found to
"support metacognition and self-regulation" and "develop critical thinking and
problem-solving abilities" through student journals and reflective writing
exercises.

Notre Dame Learning (2024) notes that "structured reflection increases the
effectiveness of learning activities and encourages students to incorporate
feedback and grades in order to harness intrinsic motivation."

## Reflection Prompt Patterns

Progress from simple to deep:

1. **Process reflection:** "What did you do? What steps did you take?"
2. **Decision reflection:** "Why did you choose this approach over the
   alternative? What trade-off did you accept?"
3. **Failure reflection:** "What didn't work? What did you learn from the
   failure? What would you do differently?"
4. **Learning reflection:** "What did you learn about your own learning
   process? What surprised you about how you approached this problem?"
5. **Transfer reflection:** "How would you apply this approach in a different
   context? What would stay the same? What would change?"

## Relationship to Existing Skills

- **Evidence-Driven Design** collects process evidence; metacognitive
  reflection collects *learning* evidence
- **CONTRIBUTIONS.md format** already includes a reflection question — this
  skill provides the patterns for designing good reflection questions
- **Pedagogical Analysis** is the instructor/muse's metacognition; this skill
  is the student's metacognition

## When to Use

- Every team exercise: at least one reflection prompt in CONTRIBUTIONS.md
- Every project assignment: a dedicated reflection section
- Graduate work: reflection as a graded component, not optional

## Risks

- **Reflection fatigue:** if every artifact requires deep reflection, students
  start producing formulaic responses. Guard by varying the reflection type and
  making some reflections optional.
- **Reflection ≠ self-assessment:** reflection is about *process*, not *grade
  negotiation*. Guard by framing prompts around learning, not performance.

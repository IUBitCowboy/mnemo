In this in-class exercise you will work individually. You may exchange brief observations with your teammates, but the strategy and its wording must be your own.

The purpose of this exercise is to design a logging strategy for your team’s Monster File Editor (MFE), applying the principles and vocabulary from this week’s logging lecture (Week 6, Day 1). Write it so your team could adopt it in the MFE Part 2 build, which is due at the end of Week 6.

 Task 

Using the markdown template below, design a logging strategy for the MFE. For each bracketed prompt, delete the prompt text and write your own answer in its place. The template’s section headings are fixed; the thinking and the wording are yours. Consult your lecture notes and the Python logging HOWTO (https://docs.python.org/3/howto/logging.html).

If a prompt exposes something your team has not decided yet, record it as an open question in the last section rather than inventing a fake answer. What you submit is a strategy — a document. No terminal transcript is required for this ICEX.

# MFE Logging Strategy — ICEX08

Course: SE1/P465
Date: [today's date]
Author: [your IU username]

## Why the MFE logs

[Name at least two distinct purposes logging serves for the MFE — drawn from the lecture's "reasons for logging" — and justify each one in MFE terms, not in generic terms.]

## What the MFE logs

[List the events the MFE should log, worked through the MFE's actual flow: starting up, reading monster.txt, the selection menu, showing current values, accepting edits, the review display, validation, and saving monster.txt-v2. Your inventory must cover each of these four kinds from the lecture: (1) program lifecycle events, (2) important program-flow points, (3) errors and unexpected flows, and (4) the save itself — saving monster.txt-v2 is the act a logging strategy most owes an audit record for. Name the events concretely, e.g. "user selected monster X from category Y", not "selection happens".]

## Log details and levels

[State the timestamp format you will use (the lecture named a standard), what origin information each record carries, and assign a logging level to at least three of the events above. Justify each level using the lecture's level definitions — a warning is for unusual events the program can work around, an error is recoverable, critical is unrecoverable. Consider: what is a validation failure that the user must fix? What is a save that failed?]

## Python logging plan

[How your team wires it up: (1) logger naming — one root logger or per-module loggers, and their names; (2) at least one handler and where records go — console, file, or both — and why that fits a terminal application; (3) the formatter fields; (4) how the active level is controlled at runtime so operational choices do not require code changes.]

## Log management

[Where records live and how growth is handled, whether logs are append-only, and whether anything logged could be sensitive. The MFE is a course tool — say in one or two sentences what your actual exposure concerns are.]

## Principles applied

[Pick at least two of the lecture's principles of logging in code and state, concretely, how each one shapes the MFE's source code — for example, how the code stays readable around the logging calls, or what "report, not analyze" forbids.]

## Team recommendations and open questions

[Name at least one logging convention your team should adopt in common for the MFE (naming, format, destination) and at least one thing you are still unsure about.]
